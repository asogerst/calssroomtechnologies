Critical Evaluation

My research and experience in the classroom showed that digital technologies can be extremely valuable in the science classroom. While they offer great opportunities to scaffold learning resources and visualise abstract scientific concepts, teachers must evaluate these benefits against ethical dilemmas, accessibility and possible distractions (Kearney & Nielsen, 2019; Buchanan, 2019). Further catches in a fast-developing field are educational hype-cycles, promising personalised learning and saving time for teachers, that have to be used with caution (Sanders & George, 2017).

My teaching pedagogy combines cognitivism with constructivism in most lessons (Bower, 2017). Digital technologies, such as PhET interactives like Build an Atom, or digital stories, such as The Adventures of Wally, are important tools to implement in the explain phase of explicit teaching (Archer & Hughes, 2011). For inquiry-based learning, online-based whiteboards or secondary research move students to active learners with agency (Bezen & Çıralı Sarıca, 2026). Once students have mastered the foundational content through explicit teaching, they can participate fully in higher-order thinking investigations (Bower, 2017). Especially in science, where students cannot conduct all experiments due to safety issues, digital simulations offer a strong alternative (Kearney & Nielsen, 2019). However, digital tools cannot just be used as an add-on, but rather as a tool to move students into higher-order thinking in scientific inquiry. Further, when pedagogy aligns with UDL principles, digital technologies become invaluable resources for differentiation. The multimodal means of representation, engagement and action and expression that websites, interactives and GenAI can offer support students with many different learning needs (CAST, 2024). Technologies, such as text-to-speech and scaffolded learning platforms, such as Stile, can be great additions to a teacher’s pedagogical repertoire (Stile Education, n.d.).

This technology integration fails if the teacher lacks the necessary digital competence. Falloon’s (2020) teacher digital competence (TDC) framework shows how the original TPACK model can be intertwined with personal-ethical competencies to effectively embed digital technologies into the classroom. A recent increasing example is personalised learning platforms, promising academic success for all students. Selwyn (2022, as cited in Wyatt-Smith & Kimber, 2024) argues that student learning processes cannot be reduced to mathematical data, which is what GenAI is based on. Human action and teachers’ professional expertise are needed to support and use AI (Jadsada et al., 2026). Schools further need to consider the equal access to technology of their students and the algorithms that underpin the subscribed AI learning platforms (Wyatt-Smith & Kimber, 2024).

The Framework for GenAI in Australian Schools provides further guidance on the safe and ethical use of these specific technologies (Wyatt-Smith & Kimber, 2024). The biggest ethical dilemmas to address are student data tracking, students’ wellbeing information and students’ own behaviour online and the associated risks of data ownership due to their lack of digital literacy (Buchanan, 2019). These have increased exponentially in recent years as students have daily access to GenAI. Students are at risk of AI dependency and AI hallucinations, which both affect the development of their critical thinking skills (Gerlich, 2025; Furze, 2025b). It is non-negotiable for teachers to explicitly teach students the general capability of digital literacy to safely manage, communicate and investigate online data (ACARA, 2026). On a global level, universities have stopped banning the use of AI and have instead implemented an AI Assessment Scale (AIAS) to preserve academic integrity (Furze et al., 2024).

The use of digital technology can be a powerful tool for the science classroom if used with intention and care. As a teacher, my role is to continually develop my digital competency and foster a classroom culture that values and expands human agency through technology, rather than replacing it. To do so, I need to collaborate with colleagues to build an “effective, trusting and caring academic culture” (Luo, 2024, as cited in Furze et al., 2024b, p. 11).

Anne G.
